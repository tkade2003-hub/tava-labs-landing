Why Founders Can't Sleep — And What It's Costing You

Why the founder brain resists shutdown, and three fixes.

It is 1:40am. The house is quiet, the laptop is closed, and your mind is running a board meeting that will not happen for another six days. If you have ever wondered why founders can't sleep even when they are exhausted, the answer is not a lack of discipline. It is biology. The same wiring that makes you good at building a company makes it remarkably hard to switch that company off at night.

This piece looks at what is actually happening in the founder brain after dark, what a steady diet of six-hour nights does to the skills you rely on most, and three changes that hold up in the research and fit around a demanding schedule.

Why founders can't sleep: the brain that will not stand down

Sleep is not something you do. It is something you allow. Falling asleep requires the nervous system to downshift from vigilance to safety, and founders spend their days training the opposite reflex.

Cortisol arrives at the wrong time

Cortisol follows a daily curve. It should peak in the first hour after waking, which is what gets you out of bed, and fall steadily until it bottoms out around midnight. Long stretches of pressure flatten and delay that curve. Late-night email, an investor call at 9pm or a cash-flow problem you are still turning over at 11 keeps cortisol elevated exactly when it should be draining away. The result is a body that is tired and a brain that is alert.

Decision fatigue does not mean a quiet mind

A founder can make hundreds of decisions in a day, most of them small and many of them unresolved. You might expect that load to leave you depleted enough to sleep. In practice, unfinished decisions behave like open tabs. The brain keeps them active, rehearsing options and consequences, because it has not been told they are handled.

The rumination loop

Rumination is the habit of replaying a problem without moving it forward. It is closely linked to delayed sleep onset and night waking. For founders the content is rarely abstract: a hire that is not working, a churned customer, a runway number. The loop feeds itself. Lying awake creates frustration, frustration raises arousal, and arousal pushes sleep further away.

Research from UC San Francisco found that 49 percent of entrepreneurs reported at least one lifetime mental health condition, a rate well above comparison groups.

None of this means something is wrong with you. It means the traits that build companies, such as vigilance, persistence and a refusal to leave problems alone, need a deliberate off switch.

What six hours a night actually does

Plenty of founders run on six hours and feel fine. The research suggests "fine" is the problem.

In a landmark study at the University of Pennsylvania, Van Dongen and colleagues restricted healthy adults to six hours in bed per night for two weeks. By the end, their attention and working memory had declined to a level comparable with people kept awake for up to two full nights. The striking part was not the decline. It was that the six-hour group rated themselves as only slightly sleepy. Their performance kept falling while their sense of how they were doing levelled off.

After two weeks of six-hour nights, cognitive performance fell to levels seen after up to two nights without any sleep, while participants felt only slightly tired.

For a founder, the casualties are the executive functions: prioritising, holding several moving parts in mind at once, inhibiting an impulsive reply, and switching cleanly between deep work and a stream of interruptions. These are the skills that separate a well-run company from a busy one, and they are among the first to suffer.

There is a visible cost too. A study published in the BMJ asked observers to rate photographs of the same people after a normal night and after sleep deprivation. The sleep-deprived faces were judged less healthy, more tired and less attractive. The people across the table notice before you do.

Three evidence-based changes for high-output schedules

The standard advice, such as no screens, a warm bath and lavender, was not written for someone fielding messages across three time zones. These three changes target the specific mechanisms above and survive contact with a real calendar.

1. Close the open loops on paper

A study at Baylor University compared two groups before bed. One spent five minutes writing about tasks they had completed. The other spent five minutes writing a specific to-do list for the coming days. The to-do group fell asleep faster, and the more specific their list, the faster they slept.

The mechanism is simple: writing a task down tells the brain it has been captured and can be released. Keep a notebook by the bed, not a phone. Before lights out, write the three things you will deal with tomorrow and the first action for each.

2. Move your cortisol curve back where it belongs

You cannot will cortisol down at 11pm, but you can shape the curve that leads there. Get outdoor light within an hour of waking, even ten minutes on an overcast day, to anchor the morning peak. Put your hardest thinking and your most adversarial meetings before mid-afternoon. Set a hard stop for high-stakes conversations two hours before bed, and treat late investor or legal calls as the exception they should be.

Caffeine belongs here too. In one sleep-lab study, caffeine taken six hours before bed still cut total sleep by more than an hour. A 3pm flat white is not a neutral choice.

3. Protect a fixed wake time, not a fixed bedtime

Founders rarely control when the day ends. They can usually control when it starts. A consistent wake time, including weekends within about an hour, stabilises your circadian rhythm faster than any bedtime rule. Build the bedtime back from it: if you need to be up at 6:30, aim to be in bed by 11:00, and protect that window the way you would protect a board meeting.

If you wake at 3am and cannot drop off within twenty minutes, get up, keep the lights low and read something dull until you feel sleepy. Lying in bed negotiating with yourself teaches the brain that bed is a place for problem-solving.

Measure it before you try to fix it

Sleep is one of the few performance levers that costs nothing, and one of the hardest to judge from the inside, because tiredness distorts your sense of how tired you are. The founders who improve fastest are the ones who track it, not the ones who rely on how they feel.
